National Park App

A UX research and app design project helping travelers plan a national park trip in one place — trails, lodging, transportation, and conditions — instead of switching between five different websites to piece one trip together.

Role
UX Research, UI Design
Type
Personal Project
Timeline
2-month self-directed project
Tools
Figma, Google Forms

The Problem

Planning a trip means juggling five tabs at once

Planning a national park trip is a challenge for travelers with limited experience because information about trails, lodging, transportation, crowds, and park conditions is scattered across multiple sources. There's no single place to go from "I want to visit Yosemite" to an actual, realistic plan.

User Goals

What travelers need from the app

  1. Plan a complete national park trip in one place, without switching between multiple websites.
  2. Discover trails and activities that match their difficulty, interests, time, and experience level.
  3. Understand where to stay and how to get around — entrances, driving times, and shuttles.
  4. Prepare for park conditions and avoid problems: weather, closures, crowds, and permits.
  5. Build and save a personalized itinerary organized by realistic distance and time.

Research

Understanding how people actually plan a trip

I wanted to understand how travelers currently plan national park trips, what resources they turn to, and where the process breaks down — before designing anything.

Target Users

  • Ages 18–45
  • People interested in visiting national parks
  • Beginner to intermediate travelers
  • People who plan their own trips using websites and apps

Hypothesis

  • Information is spread across different platforms
  • Users spend extra time comparing trails, lodging, and conditions
  • A centralized planning tool could help users feel prepared

Survey

An 18-person Google Forms survey collected data on planning resources, biggest frustrations, and most important trip information — giving a mix of beginner, some-experience, and experienced travelers.

User Interviews

Interviews with 5–6 participants explored their planning process end to end: how they chose places, trails, and lodging, and what went wrong along the way.

Competitive Analysis

Where existing apps fall short

Every existing tool solves one part of planning. I compared three of the most-used ones against the app I was designing to find the gap.

Feature AllTrails National Park Service Google Maps ParkPlan
Trail search & difficulty Excellent Limited None Yes
Park information Limited Official, thorough Limited Yes
Lodging guidance None Limited Limited Yes
Transportation & drive times None Limited Yes Yes
Crowd information Limited Limited Limited Yes
Personalized itinerary None None None Yes
Trip planning in one place None None None Yes

01 — Complement AllTrails, don't replace it

Strategy: Every trail links out to "Open in AllTrails" alongside an in-app "Add to Itinerary" button.

Goal: AllTrails already has the best trail database and a loyal user base — the opportunity isn't a better trail list, it's connecting that trail to the rest of the trip.

02 — Surface official data, minus the density

Strategy: Pull the same weather, closures, and permit information the NPS site has, but present it as scannable status chips instead of dense text pages.

Goal: Keep the trust of an official source without the "difficult for beginners" weakness research found in the NPS site.

03 — One itinerary, not five tabs

Strategy: Combine trail discovery, lodging, transportation, and conditions into a single saved itinerary.

Goal: None of the three competitors organize a trip at all — this is the gap none of them fill.

14/18 said choosing what places to visit is a top planning challenge

3.8/5 average difficulty rating for finding reliable trip information
“

Travelers juggle several resources for one trip

Participants commonly relied on Google, the National Park Service website, maps, and recommendations from friends or family — often comparing Google Maps, AllTrails, and the NPS site side by side for a single decision.

“

Drive times and logistics are underestimated

Several respondents described booking lodging that "looked close" and ending up driving over an hour each day, or not realizing how far things actually were once inside the park.

“

Crowds and parking quietly ruin plans

Avoiding crowds tied for the second-biggest planning challenge, and multiple participants specifically mentioned not knowing that parking lots fill up early.

Information Architecture

Organizing around the trip, not the topic

Research showed users struggled to find planning information because it was scattered across platforms organized by topic (a trails site, a lodging site, an official park site). Instead, the app's structure follows the user's actual journey — explore, plan, save, and manage — so related information stays grouped together under a single trip.

Scoping the MVP

Deciding what actually ships first

With a long wish list of possible features, I sorted everything by value versus effort to protect the core planning journey from scope creep — features that didn't directly help someone go from "interested" to "prepared" were pushed to a future roadmap.

Top Priority — High Value, Easy

  • Park search & overview
  • Trail search, filters & details
  • Itinerary planner
  • Save favorites

Future Roadmap — High Value, Hard

  • Crowd predictions
  • AI trip recommendations
  • Real-time weather alerts
  • Offline maps

Nice to Have — Low Value, Easy

  • Packing checklist
  • Photo gallery
  • Share itinerary

Not MVP — Low Value, Hard

  • Hotel booking
  • Social community feed
  • AR trail navigation

The MVP focuses on the core planning journey: discover a park, compare trails, find nearby lodging, and organize an itinerary. Crowd predictions and AI recommendations stayed on the roadmap — genuinely valuable, but not buildable without real-time data this project didn't have access to.

Task Analysis

From search to a saved trip

Goal

Take a user from searching a park to a saved, realistic trip plan without leaving the app.

User searches for a park and opens its overview.

User explores trails, lodging, or transportation options based on what they need.

User saves favorites as they go, then builds an itinerary from what they've saved.

User reviews the trip plan and edits it until it's realistic, then saves the trip.

Success: the user has one saved trip containing trails, lodging, and transportation — built without leaving the app or losing their place.

The Solution

Walking through ParkPlan

Every core screen answers a specific research finding directly — not a generic "trip planner" feature, but a fix for something a real participant described.

01 — Home

A trip alert before you need one

The home screen surfaces a proactive alert — "Parking lots in Yosemite Valley typically fill by 8:30AM" — directly answering the parking and crowd concerns that came up throughout research, before the user even has to ask.

02 — Current Conditions

Six scattered questions, one row of chips

Weather, crowd level, road status, shuttle status, parking, and alerts — the exact information 15 of 18 survey respondents ranked as most important — are answered together on the park page instead of across six separate searches.

03 — Trail Detail

Add it to the trip, or check AllTrails

Rather than competing with AllTrails' trail database, every trail links out to it directly while adding an "Add to Itinerary" action AllTrails doesn't have — the connective tissue competitors were missing.

04 — Itinerary

Trails, lodging, and transportation, one trip

Answers the top user goal directly: a single day-by-day itinerary that holds activities, timing, and the trip logistics (getting there, lodging, shuttles, safety) that were previously scattered across five tabs.

05 — Saved & Collections

Somewhere for "maybe later" to live

Research showed people save ideas in notes apps and screenshots long before they're ready to plan. Collections give that early, undecided browsing a home inside the app instead of scattered across other tools.

Reflection

What I'd explore next

The MVP proves out the core planning journey, but the roadmap items deferred during prioritization are where this app gets genuinely smart: real-time crowd predictions instead of typical patterns, AI-suggested itineraries based on a user's past trips, and offline maps for the parks where cell service disappears the moment you need it most.
